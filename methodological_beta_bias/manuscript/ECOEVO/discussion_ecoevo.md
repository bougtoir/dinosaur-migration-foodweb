## Discussion

### Structural comparability before ecological interpretation

When are compared assemblages structurally comparable enough for a beta-diversity contrast to be read ecologically? The simulations show that guild-level contrasts are vulnerable in more than one direction. With no generative contrast, structural asymmetry alone (unequal pools, dominance and taxonomic lumping) generated apparent contrasts of [[sim01_bias_p4|2]] for a four-taxon pool and [[sim01_bias_p2|2]] for a two-taxon pool. Temporal aggregation behaved differently: it erased the structurally generated component of a contrast ([[sim04_db_width1]] to [[sim04_db_width8]] under full pooling; [[sim13_2d_temporal_thin]] to [[sim13_2d_temporal_pooled]] on the lattice) but amplified imposed ecological contrasts. Assemblage construction can thus create, erase, exaggerate or reshape apparent spatial structure, and the sign of the distortion can depend on the source of the contrast rather than on the aggregation operation alone. None of the component mechanisms is new; what the simulations add is their magnitude under controlled conditions, their interaction, their effect on distance decay, and a demonstration of how they play out in a real dataset. The magnitudes are demonstrations of sufficiency under specified conditions, not constants to be transferred to other systems.

### Pool size and distance decay

Pool-size imbalance was the largest single source of bias. Richness effects on dissimilarity metrics are recognised (Kraft et al., 2011; Chase et al., 2011), and Jaccard dissimilarity combines species replacement with richness difference (Baselga, 2010, 2012; Legendre, 2014). The magnitudes observed here, a fully reproducible apparent contrast of [[sim01_bias_p4|2]] from a four-taxon versus 26-taxon pool with no generative difference, indicate that pool-size-matched nulls are a minimal requirement rather than an optional refinement, and the result held on a two-dimensional lattice ([[sim13_2d_bias_p4|2]] and [[sim13_2d_bias_p2|2]]). Comparisons among guilds or regions with different pool structures remain vulnerable even after standard diversity partitioning (Chase et al., 2018).

Structural asymmetry also changed the shape of turnover, not only its mean. On the lattice, the small-pool guild showed a steeper apparent distance-decay slope ([[sim13_2d_decayslope_small_p4]]) than the comparison guild ([[sim13_2d_decayslope_comparison_p4]]) under identical generative spatial processes. Distance decay is a core tool for diagnosing dispersal limitation and environmental filtering (Nekola & White, 1999; Soininen et al., 2007; Morlon et al., 2008). The result implies that differences in pool size alone can mimic differences in decay rate, so that a reported difference in decay slopes between groups may reflect differential sensitivity to pool size rather than differential dispersal. This does not mean that empirical differences in distance decay are generally artefacts; it means that decay comparisons need the same structural controls as comparisons of mean dissimilarity, for example pool-matched or richness-standardised decay curves.

### Dominance and taxonomic aggregation

A single dominant taxon shifted Δβ by approximately [[sim02_bias_pD0.5_allolike|2]] at moderate prevalence and [[sim02_bias_pD0.9|2]] at high prevalence, consistent with the known sensitivity of dissimilarity to common species (McGeoch & Gaston, 2002; Lennon et al., 2004; Hillebrand et al., 2008; Avolio et al., 2019). Genus-level pooling of geographically differentiated species produced an apparent continuity shift of [[sim03_B_lumping_k6|+3]], mirroring taxonomic-sufficiency effects documented for assemblage analyses (Lane & Benton, 2003; Bertrand et al., 2006; Bevilacqua et al., 2009). The two mechanisms are usefully distinguished. Dominance changes which sites are occupied, whereas lumping changes which entities are counted, fusing several spatially segregated species into one widespread taxon. Both inflate apparent spatial continuity, but through different data-construction paths, so the diagnostics differ: occupancy–frequency inspection and leave-one-dominant analyses for the former, and cross-resolution sensitivity tests for the latter.

### Temporal aggregation is source-dependent

Time averaging is generally expected to homogenise assemblages (Kidwell & Bosence, 1991; Olszewski, 1999; Martin, 1999; Tomašových & Kidwell, 2010). The simulations qualify this expectation for the union-pooling operator tested here. Where the finest-resolution contrast was structural, pooling erased it; where the contrast included an imposed difference in range breadth, pooling amplified it [[sim21_weak_fold|1]]- to [[sim21_strong_fold|1]]-fold ([[sim21_weak_thin|2]] to [[sim21_weak_pooled|2]], [[sim21_moderate_thin|2]] to [[sim21_moderate_pooled|2]] and [[sim21_strong_thin|2]] to [[sim21_strong_pooled|2]]). ### Sampling

An eight-fold sampling asymmetry produced comparatively small bias (approximately [[sim05_db_mult8|2]], essentially unchanged from the symmetric case) within the tested parameter range. I do not infer that sampling is unimportant; occurrence databases, including fossil databases, are demonstrably sampling-structured (Smith & McGowan, 2007; Alroy, 2008; Vilhena & Smith, 2013). The result indicates only that, under this parameterisation, the isolated sampling effect was smaller than the pool-size, dominance and lumping effects.

### Non-additive combined effects of structural factors

Because pool size, dominance, taxonomic resolution and sampling co-vary in real assemblages, the bias terms cannot be treated as independent additive corrections. In the factorial design, the combined bias was more than twice the additive expectation built from single-factor effects in the low-pool, high-dominance, intermediate-lumping region ([[sim06_bias_p4_pd0.9_k3|2]] versus [[sim06_additive_p4_pd0.9_k3|2]]), and the extreme cells reached [[sim06_max_abs_bias|2]] when lumping collapsed the whole small guild into one taxon. The latter value is a boundary case, but it illustrates the endpoint of the non-additive combination: once structural features remove all turnover from one guild, the observed contrast reflects only the other guild. Correcting only pool size leaves dominance and lumping free to generate a contrast, so diagnostics should be sequential and cross-checked rather than a single covariate adjustment.

### The Morrison worked example

In the Morrison Formation, a naive predator–herbivore Δβ of [[emp_db_naive]] lay close to, but marginally beyond, the lower bound of the gamma-matched null interval ([[emp_gamma_null_interval.lo]]), was more negative than the frequency-matched null, and collapsed to [[emp_db_minus_allosaurus]] after exclusion of the single dominant predator; species-level resolution gave [[emp_db_species]], and the dominant predator was not geographically exceptional after adjustment for duration and occurrence count (extent residual [[emp_allo_extent_resid_km|1]] km, z = [[emp_allo_extent_z|2]]). Collection covariates explained substantial presence structure in exploratory models. The appropriate interpretation is not that an ecological account of the contrast has been disproven. Rather, after structural diagnostics a strong biological explanation is no longer required to account for the observed pattern, and biological interpretation becomes non-unique. Structural sufficiency is not causal attribution: the analysis does not show that *Allosaurus* was sedentary, non-migratory or otherwise behaviourally constrained, and it does not identify a unique causal mechanism. Nemegt, analysed with the same locked specification, showed Δβ = [[nem_db_full|+3]] ([[nem_db_full.lo]] to [[nem_db_full.hi|+3]]); the Morrison direction is not a universal predator-guild property.

### General applicability

The mechanisms quantified here are relevant wherever assemblages that differ in pool size, dominance, taxonomic grain, temporal span or sampling structure are compared: museum and herbarium records, long-term monitoring compilations, multi-source biodiversity databases, archaeological faunas and palaeoecological records (Shaffer et al., 1998; Graham et al., 2004; Pyke & Ehrlich, 2010; Lavoie, 2013). The mechanisms are broadly relevant wherever assemblages differ structurally, although the magnitudes reported here are scenario-specific and should not be transported directly to other systems. Among the mechanisms examined, pool-size asymmetry has the clearest parallel outside palaeontology, because cross-guild and cross-region richness asymmetries are ubiquitous in contemporary occurrence data; temporal aggregation is most acute in archives that integrate time; and taxonomic lumping matters wherever identification effort differs among groups. The growing use of aggregated occurrence data for biodiversity-change inference (Blowes et al., 2019) makes the direction-dependence of these distortions especially consequential.

### A practical workflow

Before a guild-level beta-diversity contrast is interpreted as evidence of dispersal limitation, connectivity, environmental filtering or habitat breadth, I suggest the following recommended diagnostic sequence, to be applied as far as the data allow rather than as a validated decision rule: (1) compare gamma diversity among the groups; (2) run pool-size-matched null models; (3) inspect occupancy and dominance and run leave-one-dominant analyses; (4) harmonise taxonomic resolution and test sensitivity across levels; (5) test the effect of temporal aggregation, analysing the finest available bins; (6) inspect sampling and detection structure; (7) compare distance-decay relationships under the same structural controls, not only mean dissimilarity; (8) examine combined effects of co-occurring structural factors; and (9) interpret the contrast as evidence of ecological connectivity, mobility, filtering or habitat breadth only to the extent that it survives the applicable checks. ### Limitations

The simulators use simplified ecology and synthetic guild construction; parameter ranges were chosen to bracket plausible assemblage structures, but the reported magnitudes are scenario-specific and are not calibrated estimates for real landscapes. The one- and two-dimensional geometries do not reconstruct real landscape heterogeneity. The mechanisms interact, so the factorial map rather than the additive scaffold is the appropriate summary. All contrasts were computed with incidence-based Jaccard dissimilarity; turnover-only or abundance-based metrics differ in their sensitivity to richness difference (Baselga, 2010; Legendre, 2014) and were not evaluated systematically. Temporal-aggregation results apply to union pooling only, and the small sampling effect applies only to the tested parameterisation. Fossil data carry taxonomic and temporal limitations, and the species-level Morrison analysis depends on identification completeness. Morrison is one worked empirical case, and Nemegt is a structural comparator, not a biological replication. Structural sufficiency does not establish causal attribution: showing that a contrast can be generated structurally does not prove that it was. Conclusions about other ecological domains require direct testing with data from those domains.

### Conclusions

Assemblage structure can create, erase, exaggerate or reshape apparent spatial ecological structure, affecting both mean beta diversity and the form of distance decay. Beta-diversity contrasts should therefore not be interpreted biologically before the structural comparability of the compared assemblages has been evaluated; the workflow in Table 3 provides a practical order for doing so.
