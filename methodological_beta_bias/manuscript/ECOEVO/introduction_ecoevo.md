## Introduction

Beta diversity, the variation in species composition among sites, and the distance decay of compositional similarity are among the most widely used descriptors of spatial ecological structure (Whittaker, 1960; Nekola & White, 1999; Soininen et al., 2007; Tuomisto, 2010; Anderson et al., 2011; Moreno & Rodríguez, 2010). Pairwise dissimilarities, multiple-site indices and decay slopes are routinely read as evidence of dispersal limitation, environmental filtering, connectivity or habitat breadth (Morlon et al., 2008; Baselga, 2010). Ecologists increasingly compare these quantities among guilds, taxonomic groups, habitats, regions or data sources, asking for example whether one guild is more spatially homogeneous than another, because such contrasts promise inference about group-specific spatial processes (Heino et al., 2015; Socolar et al., 2016). All of these applications are scale-dependent: turnover varies with the grain and extent of the data (Wiens, 1989; Sfenthourakis & Panitsa, 2012; Keil et al., 2012; Barton et al., 2013). Comparisons among groups carry a further, usually implicit, assumption: that the compared assemblages are structurally comparable, so that differences in regional pool size, dominance structure, taxonomic resolution, temporal span and sampling intensity do not themselves alter measured turnover.

In practice, compared assemblages differ in exactly these properties, often several at once. A predator guild and its prey guild typically differ in pool size; a dominant species can occupy most sampled sites in one guild while the comparison guild has no such taxon; one group may be identifiable to species while another is resolvable only to genus; one dataset may integrate a decade of sampling while another pools centuries; and the two sides may have been collected under different effort regimes. Each of these structural properties is individually known to affect diversity metrics. Measured beta diversity depends on the size and composition of the regional species pool (Kraft et al., 2011; Chase et al., 2011; Pärtel et al., 2011; Zobel, 2016), and comparisons across assemblages of unequal richness are a recognised source of scale-dependent distortion (Chase et al., 2018). Dominant species and the shape of the occupancy–frequency distribution can drive dissimilarity values regardless of the underlying spatial process (McGeoch & Gaston, 2002; Lennon et al., 2004; McGill et al., 2007; Hillebrand et al., 2008). The taxonomic grain at which data are recorded alters diversity patterns (Bertrand et al., 2006; Bevilacqua et al., 2009), temporal aggregation reshapes apparent community change (Olszewski, 1999; Korhonen et al., 2010; Blowes et al., 2019), and unequal sampling effort and incomplete detection bias comparisons that are not standardised (Gotelli & Colwell, 2001; MacKenzie et al., 2002; Colwell et al., 2012; Chao et al., 2014).

What is missing from the literature is not awareness that these biases exist but their magnitudes under controlled conditions. Several questions remain largely unquantified. How large an apparent guild-level contrast can structural asymmetry alone generate when no generative contrast exists? Does structural asymmetry alter the shape of distance decay as well as mean dissimilarity? How do the mechanisms behave when they co-occur, given that treating them as independent, additive corrections is itself an untested assumption? Can temporal aggregation move a measured contrast in different directions depending on what generated it? And, practically, what sequence of diagnostics should precede the ecological interpretation of a beta-diversity contrast? Answering these questions requires a known generative baseline, which empirical assemblages cannot provide; it requires simulation.

The problem is general. Much of the biodiversity evidence now used to infer spatial structure is historically aggregated: museum and herbarium collections, long-term monitoring compilations, multi-source databases, archaeological faunas and sedimentary records are all assembled over uneven taxonomic, temporal and sampling structure (Shaffer et al., 1998; Graham et al., 2004; Pyke & Ehrlich, 2010; Lavoie, 2013). Palaeoecological assemblages lie at the extreme end of this spectrum, with small and uneven taxon pools, strong dominance, coarse taxonomic resolution and temporal averaging over long intervals (Kidwell & Bosence, 1991; Olszewski, 1999; Behrensmeyer et al., 2000; Smith & McGowan, 2007; Badgley, 2010; Vilhena & Smith, 2013). If structural bias in guild-level comparisons is consequential anywhere, it should be consequential there, which makes fossil assemblages useful empirical stress tests for a general problem of ecological inference rather than a special case with its own rules (Stigall & Lieberman, 2006; Uhen et al., 2013; Peters & McClennen, 2016).

Here I combine (i) controlled simulations in which the generative guild contrast is fixed, on a one-dimensional spatial axis and, as a robustness check, a two-dimensional lattice; (ii) an analysis of how structural asymmetry alters distance-decay slopes as well as mean dissimilarity; (iii) a factorial design that maps how structural factors interact; and (iv) an empirical worked example in which an initially strong predator–herbivore turnover contrast in dinosaur assemblages of the Late Jurassic Morrison Formation (Maidment, 2024) is decomposed stepwise under structural diagnostics, with the Late Cretaceous Nemegt Formation as a structural comparator. The guiding relation is that the observed contrast need not equal the ecological contrast whenever the compared assemblages differ in structure (Fig. 1). The contribution is quantitative and integrative rather than the discovery of new bias mechanisms. My objectives were to quantify the magnitude of structural distortion under controlled conditions, to identify conditions that create, erase or exaggerate apparent differentiation, to decompose the Morrison contrast empirically, and to derive a practical diagnostic workflow for interpreting beta-diversity contrasts in structurally heterogeneous assemblage data.
