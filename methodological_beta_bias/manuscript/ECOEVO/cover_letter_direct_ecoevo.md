Ecologists routinely interpret differences in beta diversity and distance decay as evidence about biological spatial processes: lower turnover may be read as greater connectivity or mobility, while steeper distance decay may be attributed to stronger dispersal limitation or environmental filtering. This paper asks a more fundamental question: when are the assemblages being compared structurally comparable enough for those biological interpretations to be warranted?

Regional pool size, dominance, taxonomic resolution, temporal aggregation and sampling structure routinely differ among ecological datasets. Each of these mechanisms is individually familiar, and I do not claim their discovery here. The contribution of this study is instead quantitative and integrative: it shows how large the resulting distortions can become under controlled generative conditions, how structural factors interact, how they can alter the apparent spatial form of turnover rather than only its mean, and how these problems can be diagnosed before ecological interpretation.

Using controlled one- and two-dimensional simulations, I show that unequal regional pools alone can generate substantial apparent guild differences despite identical generative spatial processes and can markedly steepen apparent distance-decay relationships. Temporal aggregation produces an especially important result: under the pooling operator examined here, it can erase a structurally generated contrast while amplifying an imposed ecological contrast approximately four-fold. Thus, persistence of a signal after aggregation is not by itself evidence of robustness. Structural factors also interact non-additively, such that correcting one source of distortion at a time may leave a substantial false difference.

The empirical analysis provides a worked example of why this matters. A strong predator–herbivore turnover contrast in Late Jurassic Morrison Formation dinosaur assemblages attenuates sharply under sequential structural diagnostics, including dominant-taxon and taxonomic-resolution checks. The conclusion is not that a particular biological explanation has been disproven, but that structural explanations become sufficient and a strong biological narrative is no longer required by the data. The Nemegt Formation provides a structural comparator in which the Morrison pattern is not reproduced.

Fossil assemblages are deliberately used as a stress test because they magnify the structural problems examined here, but the inference problem is considerably broader. Museum and herbarium collections, long-term monitoring compilations, archaeological assemblages and multi-source biodiversity databases routinely combine unequal taxonomic pools, dominance structures, resolutions, temporal spans and sampling histories. The manuscript therefore closes with a practical diagnostic workflow for evaluating structural comparability before assigning ecological meaning to beta-diversity or distance-decay contrasts.

I believe this combination of general ecological inference, transparent simulation, empirical demonstration and practical guidance is particularly well suited to the broad ecological readership of Ecology and Evolution. The paper is intended not to introduce another beta-diversity metric, but to improve how familiar metrics are interpreted when the assemblages being compared are structurally unequal.

All underlying occurrence data are publicly available through Dryad and the Paleobiology Database.
